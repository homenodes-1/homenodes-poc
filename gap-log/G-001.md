# G-001: Residential ISP terms prohibit hosting

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| ID | G-001 |
| Date | 2026-09-28 |
| Stage | Purchase (pre-purchase planning) |
| Domain | Regulatory, Liability |
| What happened | Before buying hardware, the household's internet terms were reviewed to confirm hosting was allowed. |
| Question | Can a household lawfully host third-party AI workloads on a standard residential internet connection? |
| Rules checked | Rogers Terms of Service and Acceptable Use Policy (version 2024-10). Starlink residential terms and Fair Use Policy. Vast.ai hosting documentation. |
| Outcome | No. The Rogers residential AUP prohibits running a server, providing features of the service to third parties, and commercial use. Vast.ai requires hosts to open inbound ports so renters can connect directly. Starlink residential service is limited to personal and household use and sits behind carrier-grade NAT, which blocks inbound connections. On 2026-09-28, a local internet reseller operating on the same cable network confirmed that its terms allow servers for commercial use. The node will run on a separate line from that provider. Status: resolved, pending confirmation of IP address type and inbound port access. |
| Framework note | For a typical household, the ISP's terms of service are the only rule that clearly applies to residential compute hosting, and they prohibit it. The ISP acts as a de facto regulator, but enforcement is at its discretion and hosts are unlikely to be aware of the restriction. The same physical network carries both a prohibition and a permission, depending only on which retail contract the household signs. Whether hosting is allowed is decided by the retail provider, not by the network or any public rule. The framework's regulatory and liability domains should address whether hosting requires specific connectivity terms, and who is responsible for checking. |
